# System Design Interviews

A system design interview often gives you a vague problem, such as "design a chat app", and asks you to talk through how you would build it. Companies run this interview in different ways, so this general description is the tutorial's own. This page shows what one company says about its interview, and a common way to work through one.

## In plain words

<Voices>
<Technical>

A system design question asks you to plan a whole system, not to write one function. Amazon's preparation page for its SDE II role says that software systems often need components, a place to store data, a part that makes decisions (for example business logic), and APIs or processes <Cite n={1} />.

The same page tells candidates for that role to expect at least one systems design question in the interview loop <Cite n={1} />. It says the interviewer will ask about your design, and that you should also ask questions to complete and validate it <Cite n={1} />.

Amazon lists these objectives for the system design part: practicality, accuracy, efficiency, reliability, optimization, and scalability <Cite n={1} />. It also says that it looks for a broad understanding of system design plus expertise in one area <Cite n={1} />.

</Technical>

## When you need it

<Voices>
<Technical>

The tutorial's own advice: you may meet this interview when you apply for a software role, and the format can differ between companies and between roles. Read the official hiring page of the company and the role before you prepare.

As an example, here is the SDE II process that Amazon describes on its page (accessed 2026-10-22) <Cite n={1} />. Amazon can change its process, so check the page again before you rely on this table.

| Step | What Amazon says |
|---|---|
| Online assessment | 90 minutes for two technical questions, then 20 minutes of systems design scenarios, then an 8-minute work style survey |
| System design part of the assessment | Made-up situations with simulated managers, peers, and stakeholders. You rate which actions are most and least effective. There is no whiteboard or diagram task. |
| Interview loop | Four interviews of 55 minutes each, with at least one systems design question |

</Technical>
<Layman>

The tutorial's own advice: you may meet this interview when you apply for a software role. The format can differ between companies and between roles. Read the official hiring page of the company and the role before you prepare.

As an example, this is the SDE II process that Amazon describes on its page (accessed 2026-10-22) <Cite n={1} />. Amazon can change its process. Check the page again before you rely on this list.

- **Online assessment.** 90 minutes for two technical questions, then 20 minutes of systems design scenarios, then an 8-minute work style survey.
- **System design part of the assessment.** Made-up situations with simulated managers, peers, and stakeholders. You rate which actions are most and least effective. There is no whiteboard or diagram task.
- **Interview loop.** Four interviews of 55 minutes each, with at least one systems design question.

## How it works

<Voices>
<Technical>

The steps below are a common way to work through a question. The order and the step names are the tutorial's own. Amazon's tips support parts of them, as the cites show.

```text
1. Clarify       -> who, what, how big, what is out of scope
2. Estimate      -> rough numbers for traffic and data
3. High level    -> boxes and arrows: clients, services, storage
4. Deep dive     -> one or two parts in detail
5. Trade-offs    -> other options, failure points, what you would change
```

**1. Clarify the requirements.** Amazon tells candidates to clarify the purpose of the system, ask questions to narrow the scope, and treat the interviewer as a customer <Cite n={1} />. Its sample questions include who the system is for, which features the customer expects, and which qualities the customer assumes but does not say, such as speed and security <Cite n={1} />. Write down your assumptions and build on them <Cite n={1} />.

**2. Estimate the scale.** Amazon asks what happens if the system becomes very popular: what do two times and ten times the growth do to the design <Cite n={1} />? Google's SRE workbook describes a design method that Google calls Non-Abstract Large System Design (NALSD). It says that designers should turn a whiteboard drawing into concrete resource estimates <Cite n={2} />. It also says that sound reasoning and assumptions matter more than the final numbers <Cite n={2} />. The workbook describes Google's design practice, not its interviews.

**3. Draw a high-level design.** Show the main parts and how data flows between them. Amazon says that the design depends on non-functional requirements such as load, distribution, and security, on how users and parts interact, and on data flow <Cite n={1} />. It suggests that you review scaling ideas such as load balancing, caching, sharding, microservices, and non-relational databases <Cite n={1} />. See [Sharding](/course/sharding/) in this course.

**4. Go deeper on one part.** Pick the part that carries the most risk. Amazon asks candidates to cover operations and resilience: metrics, bottlenecks, points of failure, redundancy, and logs that help debugging <Cite n={1} />.

**5. Discuss trade-offs.** If more than one design can work, Amazon asks you to compare the trade-offs, choose one, and explain why <Cite n={1} />. The NALSD method asks similar questions in two phases. First: is it possible, and can we do better? Then, at scale: is it feasible, is it resilient, and can we do better <Cite n={2} />? Google says that in practice it moves back and forth between these questions <Cite n={2} />. [The CAP theorem](/course/cap-theorem/) is one example of a trade-off to name.

</Technical>

## A real-life example

<Voices>
<Technical>

This example is the tutorial's own. The question is "Design the online exam service of a learning platform."

You first ask: how many students take an exam at the same time? Must answers save if the browser closes? Then you assume 50,000 students in one exam window and an answer save every 30 seconds. That is about 1,700 saves a second at peak.

Next you draw students, a load balancer, an exam service, and a database. For the deep dive, you pick the answer-save path, because a lost answer is the worst failure. Last, you compare a save on each click with a batch save every 30 seconds, and you name a target for the save path, as in [SLA, SLO, and SLI](/course/sla-slo-sli/).

</Technical>

## Common mistakes

<Voices>
<Technical>

These points are the tutorial's own reading of the Amazon tips.

- **Drawing before asking.** Amazon tells candidates to clarify vague requirements and to build the design on their assumptions <Cite n={1} />.
- **Hidden assumptions.** Amazon says to document all assumptions <Cite n={1} />.
- **One design with no options.** Amazon asks for a comparison of the trade-offs and a reason for the choice <Cite n={1} />.
- **Only the happy path.** Amazon asks for failure points, redundancy, and logs, not only the customer's problem <Cite n={1} />.
- **Exact numbers too early.** The NALSD chapter says that perfect assumptions are not required <Cite n={2} />.

## What a tester can check

<Voices>
<Technical>

<Callout type="tip" title="Tutorial advice">
These questions are the tutorial's own advice, not a claim of the sources. Ask them of any design, in an interview or in a design review.

- **Who uses it, and how many?** Expected result: the design states the users and a peak load number.
- **What happens at ten times the load?** Expected result: the design names the first part that breaks and how it scales.
- **What happens when one part fails?** Expected result: each failure has a planned behavior, such as a retry, a fallback, or a clear error.
- **How do we know it works in production?** Expected result: the design names its metrics, logs, and a target for each key path.
- **Which requirement is out of scope?** Expected result: the scope is written down, so the tests match it.
</Callout>

</Technical>

## Key words

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| System design interview | An interview question about how to build a whole software system (the tutorial's definition). Amazon says to expect one in its SDE II loop <Cite n={1} /> |
| Interview loop | In Amazon's SDE II process, the set of four interviews of 55 minutes each <Cite n={1} /> |
| Requirement | What the customer expects the system to do, found by asking questions <Cite n={1} /> |
| Non-functional requirement | A quality of the system, such as load, distribution, or security <Cite n={1} /> |
| Assumption | A fact that you choose to accept and write down, so the design can build on it <Cite n={1} /> |
| Estimate | A number for the resources that a design needs, such as a count of machines <Cite n={2} /> |
| Trade-off | What you give up when you choose one design over another (the tutorial's definition). Amazon asks candidates to compare them <Cite n={1} /> |
| Bottleneck | A part that limits the whole system (the tutorial's definition). Amazon asks candidates to consider them <Cite n={1} /> |
| NALSD | Non-Abstract Large System Design, Google's iterative method to design systems with concrete resource estimates <Cite n={2} /> |
