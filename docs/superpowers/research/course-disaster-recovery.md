## Claims

| # | Claim on the page | Source | Quote |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | DR measures time to recovery after large events that count as disasters, and it has its own objectives, different from those of availability. | S4 | "Disaster recovery has different objectives from Availability, measuring time to recovery after the larger scale events that qualify as disasters." |
| C2 | A disaster is a rare, major event with a larger and longer impact than HA design can handle. Examples: natural disasters, human error with major impact (deleting production data), major security incidents (ransomware). | S5 | "a distinct, uncommon, major event that has a larger and longer-lasting impact than an application can mitigate through the high availability aspect of its design" / "Human errors that result in a major impact, such as accidentally deleting production data" / "ransomware attacks that lead to data corruption" |
| C3 | RTO: maximum acceptable delay between the interruption of service and its restoration. | S1 | "is the maximum acceptable delay between the interruption of service and restoration of service" |
| C4 | RPO: maximum acceptable time since the last data recovery point; it sets the acceptable data loss. | S1 | "is the maximum acceptable amount of time since the last data recovery point. This objective determines what is considered an acceptable loss of data" |
| C5 | Azure: RPO and RTO are in units of time ("30 minutes of data", "eight hours of downtime"). Each component or flow can have its own values. | S5 | "RPO is measured in units of time, such as "30 minutes of data" or "four hours of data."" / "Each component or flow in the workload might have individual RPO and RTO values." |
| C6 | Aiming at zero RTO and RPO is tempting but hard and costly. Business and technical people should agree on realistic values. | S5 | "While it's tempting to aim for an RTO and RPO of zero ... in practice it's difficult and costly to implement." |
| C7 | Recovery objectives should not be set alone: the chance of a disruption and the cost of recovery also matter. A business impact analysis shows how fast a workload must return and how much data loss is acceptable. | S1 | "recovery objectives should not be made in isolation; the probability of disruption and cost of recovery are key factors" / "The analysis should help to determine how quickly the workload needs to be made available and how much data loss can be tolerated." |
| C8 | If recovery costs more than the failure or loss, do not put the option in place unless there is another driver such as regulation. | S1 | "If the cost of the recovery strategy is higher than the cost of the failure or loss, the recovery option should not be put in place unless there is a secondary driver such as regulatory requirements." |
| C9 | For less critical workloads, no DR can be a valid choice. For some scenarios it is valid as an informed decision when the chance is low. | S1 | "For less critical workloads, a valid strategy may be not to have any disaster recovery in place at all." |
| C10 | The DR plan should be part of the business continuity plan (BCP), not a stand-alone document; the earthquake example (transport of products). | S1 | "Your disaster recovery plan should be a subset of your organization's business continuity plan (BCP), it should not be a standalone document." |
| C11 | HA focuses on components of the workload. DR focuses on separate copies of the whole workload, and on time to recover after large events. | S4 | "Availability focuses on components of the workload, whereas disaster recovery focuses on discrete copies of the entire workload." |
| C12 | Replication for HA can copy a deletion or corruption to the second site, so a point-in-time backup is also needed for DR. | S4 | "those destructive changes can be replicated to the secondary storage device ... a point-in-time backup is also required as part of a DR strategy." |
| C13 | Azure: a full region outage is a DR risk for a one-region workload, but an HA risk for a multi-region active-active workload with full replication and automatic failover. | S5 | "some risks can be classified as HA for one workload and DR for another workload." |
| C14 | AWS groups the strategies into four approaches, from low cost and low complexity (backups) to more complex multi-Region strategies. Active/passive uses an active site for traffic; the passive site does not serve traffic until failover. | S2 | "four approaches, ranging from the low cost and low complexity of making backups to more complex strategies using multiple active Regions" / "The passive site ... does not actively serve traffic until a failover event is triggered." |
| C15 | For the loss of one data center in a well-architected, highly available workload, backup and restore may be enough. If a disaster means a whole Region, or regulation requires it, consider pilot light, warm standby, or multi-site active/active. | S2 | "you may only require a backup and restore approach" / "you should consider Pilot Light, Warm Standby, or Multi-Site Active/Active." |
| C16 | Backup and restore: suits data loss or corruption; can cover a regional disaster by copying data to another Region. You must also redeploy infrastructure, configuration, and code. | S2 | "suitable approach for mitigating against data loss or corruption" / "In addition to data, you must redeploy the infrastructure, configuration, and application code in the recovery Region." |
| C17 | Use infrastructure as code (IaC). Without it, restore can be complex, recovery times longer, and may exceed the RTO. | S2 | "Without IaC, it may be complex to restore workloads in the recovery Region, which will lead to increased recovery times and possibly exceed your RTO." |
| C18 | How often the backup runs sets the achievable recovery point, which should match the RPO. | S2 | "How often you run your backup will determine your achievable recovery point (which should align to meet your RPO)." |
| C19 | Backups copied to the DR Region must be restored at failover time. Restoring from backup is a control plane operation, so a regular restore gives data stores ready from a recent backup if the operation is not available in a disaster. | S2 | "Any data stored in the disaster recovery Region as backups must be restored at time of failover." / "Implementing a scheduled periodic data restore is a good idea as data restore from backup is a control plane operation." |
| C20 | Pilot light: data is replicated to another Region and a copy of core infrastructure is provisioned. Data stores are always on. App servers have code and configuration but are switched off until a test or failover. | S2 | "you replicate your data from one Region to another and provision a copy of your core workload infrastructure." / "Other elements, such as application servers, are loaded with application code and configurations, but are "switched off"" |
| C21 | Pilot light minimizes ongoing cost by minimizing active resources, and simplifies recovery since core infrastructure is in place. Unlike backup and restore, core infrastructure is always available. You switch on and scale out. | S2 | "A pilot light approach minimizes the ongoing cost of disaster recovery by minimizing the active resources, and simplifies recovery at the time of a disaster" |
| C22 | Pilot light needs a change in deployment: changes go to each Region together; automate with IaC. | S2 | "You need to make core infrastructure changes to each Region and deploy workload (configuration, code) changes simultaneously to each Region." |
| C23 | Continuous replication does not protect against corruption or destruction unless there is versioning or point-in-time recovery. | S2 | "it may not protect you against data corruption or destruction unless your strategy also includes versioning of stored data or options for point-in-time recovery." |
| C24 | Warm standby: a scaled-down but fully functional copy of production in another Region, always on. It extends pilot light and shortens recovery time. It also makes (continuous) testing easier. | S2 | "a scaled down, but fully functional, copy of your production environment in another Region" / "decreases the time to recovery because your workload is always-on in another Region" / "more easily perform testing or implement continuous testing" |
| C25 | Pilot light vs warm standby: pilot light cannot process requests without extra action; warm standby can handle traffic at reduced capacity at once. Pilot light needs turn on and scale up; warm standby only scale up. Use RTO and RPO to choose. | S2 | "pilot light cannot process requests without additional action taken first, whereas warm standby can handle traffic (at reduced capacity levels) immediately." |
| C26 | Warm standby that relies on auto scaling depends on a control plane activity, which lowers resilience. Alternative: provision full capacity (hot standby), at higher cost. Check service quotas in the DR Region. | S2 | "Because Auto Scaling is a control plane activity, taking a dependency on it will lower the resiliency of your overall recovery strategy. It is a trade-off." / "Ensure that service quotas in your DR Region are set high enough" |
| C27 | Multi-site active/active: runs in several Regions at once and serves traffic from all; hot standby serves traffic from one Region. Active/active is the most complex and costly; it can cut recovery time to near zero for most disasters; data corruption may need backups, so the recovery point is usually not zero. | S2 | "This approach is the most complex and costly approach to disaster recovery, but it can reduce your recovery time to near zero for most disasters" / "data corruption may need to rely on backups, which usually results in a non-zero recovery point" |
| C28 | If you do not want to use both Regions for traffic, warm standby is more economical and operationally less complex than active/active. | S2 | "Warm Standby offers a more economical and operationally less complex approach." |
| C29 | Active/active has no failover; DR tests check how the workload reacts to loss of a Region: traffic routed away; other Regions can take all traffic. Data disaster tests are also needed. Backup and recovery are still required and tested. | S2 | "there is no such thing as failover in this scenario. Disaster recovery testing in this case would focus on how the workload reacts to loss of a Region: Is traffic routed away from the failed Region? Can the other Region(s) handle all the traffic?" |
| C30 | Failover can start automatically or manually. Automatic failover from health checks or alarms should be used with caution: recovery time and point are greater than zero, and a false alarm causes those losses. Manual start is common, but automate the steps like a button. | S2 | "Automatically initiated failover based on health checks or alarms should be used with caution." / "you should still automate the steps for failover, so that the manual initiation is like the push of a button." |
| C31 | Use data plane operations for failover where possible, because data planes typically have higher availability design goals than control planes. | S2 | "For maximum resiliency, you should use only data plane operations as part of your failover operation." |
| C32 | Regularly assess and test the DR strategy so you have confidence to invoke it. | S2 | "It is critical to regularly assess and test your disaster recovery strategy so that you have confidence in invoking it" |
| C33 | Test the DR implementation and test failover regularly, to check that RTO and RPO are met. | S3 | "regularly test failover to your workload's DR Region to ensure that RTO and RPO are met." |
| C34 | Avoid recovery paths that are rarely run. Example: a secondary data store may have too little capacity, or service quotas may be too low, when you last tested. Few recovery paths are best. | S3 | "A pattern to avoid is developing recovery paths that are rarely executed." / "The capacity of the secondary ... might no longer be able to tolerate the load under this scenario, or service quotas in the secondary Region might not be sufficient." / "having a small number of recovery paths is best" |
| C35 | For a complex or critical recovery path, still run the failure in production regularly. | S3 | "you still need to regularly execute that failure in production to validate that the recovery path works." |
| C36 | Manage configuration drift in the DR Region: infrastructure, data, configuration as needed; check AMIs and service quotas. | S3 | "Manage configuration drift at the DR Region. ... check that AMIs and service quotas are up-to-date." |
| C37 | A backup strategy must include testing the backups. | S2 | "Your backup strategy must include testing your backups." |
| C38 | Azure: a DR plan should be well defined and testable. DR is not automatic in Azure; services give features to support it. | S5 | "create a well-defined and testable DR plan" / "DR isn't an automatic feature of Azure." |
| C39 | Azure: a plan should have a runbook, a communication plan, and an escalation path. | S5 | "every DR plan should be documented to include a clear runbook, a well-defined communication plan, and a structured escalation path" |
| C40 | Azure: failover usually takes some time to detect the failure and switch (page: "It usually takes some time"); align the RTO with failover time. Failback (return to the primary Region) can be complex; data may be written after failover begins. | S5 | "Make sure that the RTO of the workload is aligned with the failover time." / "data in the primary region may have been written after failover begins" |
| C41 | Azure: backups should be stored apart from main data. Restoring usually involves data loss, since backups are taken infrequently; align the RPO with the backup interval. Restore takes time; test backups and restores; the RTO must include restore time. | S5 | "backup restoration usually involves data loss ... make sure that the workload RPO is aligned with the backup interval." / "it's critical to test your backups and restoration processes to verify their integrity and understand how long the restoration process takes" |
| C42 | Azure: backup recovery should be a last resort; the plan should say which steps to try first. | S5 | "backup recovery should be used as a last resort" |
| C43 | Azure: IaC (Bicep, ARM templates, Terraform) reduces recovery time and error compared with manual deployment. | S5 | "Using IaC reduces your recovery time and potential for error, compared to manually deploying and configuring resources." |
| C44 | Azure: routinely test DR plans; include human processes, not only technical ones; untested recovery is more likely to have major problems; tests can validate the RTO. | S5 | "Include all of the human processes in your drills, and don't just focus on the technical processes." / "by testing your DR plans and required processes, you can validate the feasibility of your RTO" |
