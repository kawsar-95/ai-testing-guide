## Claims

| # | Claim on the page | Source | Quote |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | An SLI is a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the service level. Common SLIs: request latency, error rate, throughput. | S1 Indicators | "a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the level of service that is provided." / "request latency ... Other common SLIs include the error rate ... and system throughput" |
| C2 | An SLO is a target value or range of values for a service level measured by an SLI. Structure: SLI <= target, or lower bound <= SLI <= upper bound. | S1 Objectives | "a target value or range of values for a service level that is measured by an SLI. A natural structure for SLOs is thus SLI ≤ target, or lower bound ≤ SLI ≤ upper bound." |
| C3 | An SLA is an explicit or implicit contract with users with consequences of meeting or missing the SLOs in it; the consequence is most easily seen when financial (a rebate or a penalty). | S1 Agreements | "an explicit or implicit contract with your users that includes consequences of meeting (or missing) the SLOs they contain. The consequences are most easily recognized when they are financial—a rebate or a penalty" |
| C4 | Test to tell SLO from SLA: ask what happens if the SLOs are not met; no explicit consequence means almost certainly an SLO. | S1 Agreements | "ask "what happens if the SLOs aren’t met?": if there is no explicit consequence, then you are almost certainly looking at an SLO." |
| C5 | People often say "SLA" when they mean SLO; "SLA violation" nearly always means a missed SLO. | S1 footnote 16 | "Most people really mean SLO when they say "SLA." ... if somebody talks about an "SLA violation," they are almost always talking about a missed SLO." |
| C6 | Availability is an SLI: the fraction of time a service is usable, often defined as the fraction of well-formed requests that succeed. | S1 Indicators | "availability, or the fraction of the time that a service is usable. It is often defined in terms of the fraction of well-formed requests that succeed" |
| C7 | Whether or not a service has an SLA, it is valuable to define SLIs and SLOs. Google Search has no public SLA. | S1 Agreements | "Whether or not a particular service has an SLA, it’s valuable to define SLIs and SLOs and use them to manage the service." |
| C8 | Without an explicit SLO users form their own beliefs; this can lead to over-reliance or under-reliance. Publishing SLOs sets expectations. | S1 Objectives | "Without an explicit SLO, users often develop their own beliefs about desired performance ... over-reliance ... and under-reliance" |
| C9 | SLOs help decide what engineering work to prioritize; they help balance new features against reliability work. | S2 Why SREs Need SLOs | "SLOs are a tool to help determine what engineering work to prioritize." / "a well-thought-out and adopted SLO is key to making data-informed decisions about the opportunity cost of reliability work" |
| C10 | SLIs and SLOs are used in a control loop: measure SLIs, compare to SLOs, decide if action is needed, act. Without an SLO you would not know whether or when to act. | S1 Control Measures | "Monitor and measure the system’s SLIs. Compare the SLIs to the SLOs, and decide whether or not action is needed." / "Without the SLO, you wouldn’t know whether (or when) to take action." |
| C11 | Start from what users care about, not what you can measure; what users care about may need approximating. | S1 Objectives in Practice | "Start by thinking about (or finding out!) what your users care about, not what you can measure." |
| C12 | Do not use every metric as an SLI; too many hides the key ones, too few leaves behavior unexamined; a handful is typically enough. | S1 What Do You and Your Users Care About? | "You shouldn’t use every metric you can track in your monitoring system as an SLI" / "a handful of representative indicators are enough" |
| C13 | Typical SLIs by service type: serving systems - availability, latency, throughput; storage - latency, availability, durability; big data - throughput, end-to-end latency; all - correctness. | S1 same | "User-facing serving systems ... availability, latency, and throughput." / "Storage systems often emphasize latency, availability, and durability." / "Big data systems ... throughput and end-to-end latency" / "All systems should care about correctness" |
| C14 | Workbook advice: treat the SLI as good events divided by total events, 0% to 100%. Examples: successful HTTP requests / all HTTP requests. | S2 What to Measure | "we generally recommend treating the SLI as the ratio of two numbers: the number of good events divided by the total number of events." / "The SLI ranges from 0% to 100%, where 0% means nothing works, and 100% means nothing is broken." |
| C15 | Workbook advice: pick a small number (five or fewer) of SLI types for the most critical functions. | S2 A Worked Example | "We recommend choosing a small number (five or fewer) of SLI types that represent the most critical functionality to your customers." |
| C16 | Client-side measurement can reveal problems that server-side metrics miss (example: page JavaScript). | S1 Collecting Indicators | "not measuring behavior at the client can miss a range of problems that affect users but don’t affect server-side metrics." |
| C17 | For a first SLI, choose something that needs little engineering work, such as existing web server logs. | S2 Moving from SLI Specification to SLI Implementation | "For your first SLIs, choose something that requires a minimum of engineering work. If your web server logs are already available ... use the logs." |
| C18 | Averages hide the long tail. Example: typical request about 50 ms but 5% are 20 times slower; alerting on the average would show no change. | S1 Aggregation | "A simple average can obscure these tail latencies" / "although a typical request is served in about 50 ms, 5% of requests are 20 times slower!" |
| C19 | A high percentile (99th, 99.9th) shows a plausible worst case; the 50th (median) shows the typical case. | S1 Aggregation | "a high-order percentile, such as the 99th or 99.9th, shows you a plausible worst-case value, while using the 50th percentile (also known as the median) emphasizes the typical case." |
| C20 | Several grades of SLO capture both the typical user and the long tail. Example: 90% of requests faster than 100 ms and 99% faster than 400 ms. | S2 A Worked Example | "we also recommend using multiple grades of SLOs ... 90% of requests are faster than 100 ms, and 99% of requests are faster than 400 ms." |
| C21 | An SLO should say how it is measured and when it is valid. | S1 Defining Objectives | "SLOs should specify how they’re measured and the conditions under which they’re valid." |
| C22 | 100% is unrealistic and undesirable as an SLO: it can slow innovation and deployment, need expensive over-conservative solutions, or both. Instead allow an error budget: a rate at which the SLOs may be missed. Track daily or weekly. | S1 Defining Objectives | "It’s both unrealistic and undesirable to insist that SLOs will be met 100% of the time: doing so can reduce the rate of innovation and deployment, require expensive, overly conservative solutions, or both. Instead, it is better to allow an error budget—a rate at which the SLOs can be missed—and track that on a daily or weekly basis." |
| C23 | The violation rate is compared with the error budget; the gap feeds the decision on when to roll out new releases. | S1 Defining Objectives | "The SLO violation rate can be compared against the error budget ..., with the gap used as an input to the process that decides when to roll out new releases." |
| C24 | Reasons the workbook gives against 100%: failures have a nonzero probability even with redundancy; users are behind a long chain of components; each extra nine costs more while the benefit to customers approaches zero; change is the top source of outages, so a 100% target blocks updates. | S2 Reliability Targets and Error Budgets | "there is a nonzero probability that one or more components will fail simultaneously" / "each extra nine comes at an increased cost, but the marginal utility to your customers steadily approaches zero." / "The number one source of outages is change" |
| C25 | Error budget in the workbook: budget = 100% minus SLO. Example: 99.9% success SLO, 3 million requests in four weeks, budget 3,000 errors; an outage with 1,500 errors costs 50%. | S2 What to Measure | "the SLO is a target percentage and the error budget is 100% minus the SLO. For example, if you have a 99.9% success ratio SLO, then a service that receives 3 million requests over a four-week period had a budget of 3,000 (0.1%) errors over that period. If a single outage is responsible for 1,500 errors, that error costs 50% of the error budget." |
| C26 | An error budget policy says what to do when the budget is used up. Common actions: stop feature launches until back in SLO, or put some or all engineering time on reliability. | S2 Decision Making / Establishing an Error Budget Policy | "Common policies include stopping feature launches until the service is once again within SLO or devoting some or all engineering time to working on reliability-related bugs." |
| C27 | Stakeholders (product manager, developers, the team that runs the service) should agree on the SLO. | S2 Getting Stakeholder Agreement | "you will need to get all stakeholders to agree to it" |
| C28 | Window: rolling windows match user experience; calendar windows match business planning; four-week rolling window is a good general-purpose choice. | S2 Choosing an Appropriate Time Window | "Rolling windows are more closely aligned with user experience" / "Calendar windows are more closely aligned with business planning and project work." / "We have found a four-week rolling window to be a good general-purpose interval." |
| C29 | Do not pick a target only from current performance (book). The workbook says current performance can be a starting point if you iterate. First SLO need not be correct. | S1 Choosing Targets; S2 What to Measure | "Don’t pick a target based on current performance" / "your current performance can be a good place to start if you don’t have any other information, and if you have a good process for iterating in place" / "Your first attempt at an SLI and SLO doesn’t have to be correct" |
| C30 | Have as few SLOs as possible. | S1 Choosing Targets | "Have as few SLOs as possible" / "Choose just enough SLOs to provide good coverage" |
| C31 | Safety margin: a tighter internal SLO than the advertised one gives room to react before users see the problem. | S1 SLOs Set Expectations | "Using a tighter internal SLO than the SLO advertised to users gives you room to respond to chronic problems before they become visible externally." |
| C32 | Do not overachieve: users rely on actual performance. Chubby (Google's lock service) had planned outages so that it met but did not greatly exceed its SLO. | S1 SLOs Set Expectations; Chubby box | "If your service’s actual performance is much better than its stated SLO, users will come to rely on its current performance." / "SRE makes sure that global Chubby meets, but does not significantly exceed, its service level objective." |
| C33 | SRE usually does not build SLAs (business and product decisions) but helps define SLIs so the SLOs can be measured objectively. Business and legal teams pick the consequences. | S1 Agreements; Agreements in Practice | "SRE doesn’t typically get involved in constructing SLAs, because SLAs are closely tied to business and product decisions." / "Crafting an SLA requires business and legal teams to pick appropriate consequences and penalties for a breach." |
